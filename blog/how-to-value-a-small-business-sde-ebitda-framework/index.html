SDE and EBITDA: Review the Earnings Bridge Before Valuation

By Dr. Connor Robertson Published June 17, 2026Reviewed November 22, 2026

Before using SDE or EBITDA in a business valuation, establish what the earnings number includes and reconcile it to the underlying records. Then identify the business interest, valuation date, operating assumptions, and method being considered. An asking price or a headline multiple cannot answer those questions on its own.

This guide explains an earnings-review process and a fictional reconciliation. It does not provide a valuation opinion, a market multiple, a lending decision, or a recommendation to buy a business.

Define the measures · Fictional earnings bridge · Review adjustments · Capital spending and cash · Connect to valuation

Define SDE, EBITDA, and any further adjustments

EBITDA means earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation, and amortization. For the simple example below, the starting point is net income and the tax adjustment is income-tax expense. Payroll, sales, and other operating taxes are not automatically added back because the measure contains the word “taxes.” Identify the accounts and accounting basis actually used.

When a presentation also removes items such as transaction expenses or owner-related costs, ask for the definition and a separate reconciliation of those adjustments. A label such as “adjusted EBITDA” does not establish that every adjustment is supported, nonrecurring, or appropriate for the buyer's operating plan.

Seller's discretionary earnings, or SDE, is used in owner-operated business discussions to describe an owner-benefit earnings measure. Request the preparer's precise definition: whose compensation is included, which benefits and expenses were already recorded, which owner roles are assumed to continue, and which adjustments are proposed. Do not apply an assumed formula to every seller's presentation.

A fictional earnings bridge: the owner still does work

These invented annual figures illustrate the distinction between accounting earnings, an owner-benefit presentation, and an operating plan that hires replacement management. They are not actual company results, typical compensation, a recommended adjustment schedule, or a valuation.

The $350,000 presentation and the $160,000 scenario describe different assumptions about who performs the work. A buyer who operates the business personally still supplies time and capability. A buyer who hires a manager needs a supported staffing plan. Neither case establishes what multiple or price should apply.

Review each proposed add-back individually

For each adjustment, record the amount, account, entity, period, source document, reason proposed, expected treatment under the buyer's plan, and review status. Preserve rejected and unresolved adjustments alongside accepted ones so the bridge remains reproducible.

  • A cost must be in the starting earnings before it can be removed from those earnings. Do not add back a balance-sheet purchase or an owner distribution as though it were an expense in net income.
  • Count an expense once. An owner benefit included in a compensation total should not also appear as a separate discretionary add-back.
  • Identify continuing work and obligations. Removing a seller's salary or a family member's payroll cost does not remove the tasks they perform. Review the replacement plan and its cost.
  • Support a nonrecurring classification. A one-time description needs evidence and context. Repeated projects, repairs, turnover, or professional fees may indicate continuing needs even when individual invoices differ.
  • Separate accounting and tax questions. A seller calling an expense “personal” does not establish its accounting treatment, deductibility, or appropriate valuation adjustment. Route those issues to the responsible reviewers.

A proposed $10,000 adjustment with missing support remains unresolved; it does not increase the supported earnings measure merely because it appears on a seller's schedule. Keep a separate scenario if the team wants to understand its possible effect.

Account for replacement equipment and cash timing separately

Depreciation is an accounting expense; buying replacement equipment is a cash outlay with its own accounting treatment and timing. An EBITDA bridge that adds back depreciation does not demonstrate that future maintenance or replacement spending is zero.

If an equipment purchase was capitalized, its full purchase cost was not necessarily included as an expense in the net-income starting point. Adding the purchase price to earnings would therefore require examination of the actual accounting entries and could double count an amount. Review the income statement, fixed-asset records, depreciation, and cash flows together.

In the fictional business above, suppose the operating plan also identifies a $60,000 equipment replacement. Record that amount and its expected payment date in the cash plan. The earnings bridge alone cannot determine whether sufficient cash will exist when payment is due. Working-capital changes, taxes, transaction costs, interest, principal payments, and other commitments require their own analysis.

Use the cash-before-first-payroll guide for a separate timing review. Keep earnings, purchase funding, and post-close operating liquidity visible as distinct records.

Match the measure to the valuation question

Specify the interest or assets being considered, the effective date, purpose, information available, assumptions, and limitations. Ask the valuation professional to explain which approaches are appropriate and why. An income approach, market approach, and asset-based approach answer the valuation problem through different analyses; a single earnings multiple is not a substitute for that judgment.

Where a multiple or capitalization rate is used, ask whether it matches the selected benefit stream and whether the comparison data are relevant to the subject business. The numerator, denominator, ownership interest, included assets and liabilities, and transaction terms must be understood before comparing two quoted results.

Distinguish an indication of operating-business value from what a buyer would pay for the particular interest under negotiated terms. Cash, debt, working-capital arrangements, excluded assets, contingent payments, and other terms need an explicit bridge where relevant. The bridge and its treatment depend on the transaction and valuation scope; this guide does not supply a universal price formula.

Document what remains unknown and which review is needed before the next decision. Completing an earnings worksheet supports a better question; it does not certify the seller's records or approve a purchase.

Primary references and scope

The SEC staff's non-GAAP financial-measure interpretations, including Question 103.01, address EBITDA definitions and reconciliation in SEC reporting. They provide background for distinguishing measures here; this article does not conclude that a private-business presentation is subject to a particular SEC filing requirement.

The IRS's Internal Revenue Manual business-valuation guidelines discuss defining the assignment, supporting information, valuation approaches, and consistency between benefit streams and methods. Those guidelines address IRS personnel. The review process and fictional figures here are original educational material, not an IRS valuation, approval, or general compliance determination.
