Organize Acquisition Document Questions by Source and Review Status

By Buying Wealth Editorial July 1, 2026 Document review questions

A folder of acquisition documents is a starting point for questions. Receiving a file does not establish that its contents are complete, current, applicable to the proposed transaction or reviewed by the person responsible for that subject. A working review note helps keep those states separate.

Give each question a specific source

Instead of writing “financials checked,” identify the file, version, period and particular question. What does the supplied document describe? Who supplied it? Which period does it cover? Which figures or statements require explanation? Keep the source wording beside the question so a later reader can distinguish the record from an interpretation.

A filename such as final or approved is a label to investigate. Record who assigned that status and what it means in this context. A label alone does not establish the accuracy of the figures or authorization for a transaction. Avoid replacing a missing period or unidentified author with an assumption.

Separate receipt, inspection and review

Use a small set of explicit status descriptions: requested, received, opened, reviewed for a named question, and awaiting clarification. Define the descriptions within the note. Opening a spreadsheet is different from examining its formulas, underlying records or reconciliation. A reviewer may have answered one question while other questions about the same file remain open.

For each review, record the person, the scope they accepted and the evidence they actually considered. A name beside a document may be a proposed assignment. Confirm acceptance separately. When a question involves accounting, agreements, tax treatment or regulatory requirements, identify the relevant adviser and the actual materials they need; this article supplies no rule about liability, transaction structure or compliance.

Compare records on an identified basis

Two reports can use similar headings while describing different periods, populations or definitions. Before treating a difference as an error, record those fields and ask whether the comparison is like for like. A changed figure does not, by itself, identify a motive or establish misrepresentation.

Keep the earlier version when recording a later explanation. Note which value changed, which question the explanation addresses and whether the explanation has been reviewed. The existence of a revised document neither proves that the first document was reliable nor explains why the revision occurred. Preserve that uncertainty until there is supporting evidence.

Write a question that someone can answer

“Is everything fine?” hides several separate tasks. A more concrete original prompt is: “Which record supports this figure for this period, and which differences from the supplied summary remain unexplained?” Another is: “Which version of this agreement was included in the review, and which question was the reviewer asked to address?” These prompts organize an enquiry; they do not certify a transaction or establish the legal meaning of an agreement.

Attach the relevant identifier or location within the file. Keep a question about document contents separate from a question about an action somebody proposes to take. A response explaining a record is not automatically permission to contact an employee, customer or counterparty, share restricted material, change a process or proceed with a purchase.

A fictional document-review note

Suppose an invented file EX-DOC-4 is described as a March summary. Mira has received it, but its underlying transaction list has not been supplied. Owen has agreed to review whether the summary and list use the same period once the list arrives. The note can say “summary received; supporting list requested; period comparison pending.” It cannot say “earnings verified” or “review complete.” Mira and Owen are fictional names, and this is no report of an actual acquisition.

If a later version EX-DOC-4B arrives, add its identifier and the stated explanation. Preserve the earlier review question. Ask whether the reviewer has considered the new version and whether the missing list is now available. A new filename should not silently close an unresolved question.

Make the next review traceable

End each open item with the evidence requested, proposed contact, response status and next review point. Distinguish a proposed date from an agreed commitment. Record any deadline from the actual governing source and ask the responsible reviewer about its meaning; no universal review duration or document lookback period is recommended here.

A completed note establishes what was recorded within its stated scope. It does not show that every relevant document was found, that a business is worth a particular price or that a buyer should close, renegotiate or withdraw. Those conclusions require their own evidence and responsible review.

These are original editorial questions about organizing a document review. They are not a book quotation, a chapter method, a buyer anecdote, a professional opinion or a report of a live transaction. Document review questions

Does receiving every requested file mean due diligence is complete?

No. Receipt does not establish completeness, applicability, accuracy or review. Record which questions were addressed and what remains unresolved.

What should a note record about a revised document?

Identify both versions, the stated explanation, the question affected and whether the responsible reviewer has considered the revision. Preserve unanswered questions.

Does a reviewer name establish an accepted assignment?

No. Record whether the person accepted the scope and which materials they actually reviewed.

Can a document checklist determine whether to buy a business?

No. A checklist records tasks within its stated scope. It does not establish valuation, legal consequences or an appropriate transaction decision.
