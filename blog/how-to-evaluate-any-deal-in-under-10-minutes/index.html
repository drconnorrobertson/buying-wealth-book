Business Acquisition Screening: Five Questions Before Diligence

By Dr. Connor Robertson Published April 22, 2026 · Reviewed November 22, 2026

An initial acquisition screen organizes the information available and identifies the next decision. Its useful output is a short record of fit, supporting evidence, unresolved questions, and the work needed next. A brief conversation or a listing review cannot establish that a business is worth buying.

Write down the buyer's current acquisition criteria before applying these questions. Keep a stated requirement separate from a preference and from a claim that still needs verification. If a requirement changes, record the new version and the reason.

Five screening questions · Screening decisions · Fictional examples · Screening worksheet

Five questions for the initial review

1. What supports the current earnings and cash picture?

Identify the entity, period, and source behind the revenue, expenses, and earnings supplied. Distinguish a listing description from financial statements and from a completed independent review. Note which records are missing and who would review them.

Keep historical earnings separate from the proposed buyer's cash obligations. Debt terms, payment dates, working-capital needs, capital spending, taxes, and transaction costs affect different parts of the plan. A positive earnings figure alone cannot establish that the buyer has enough operating cash. Use the SDE and EBITDA earnings review and cash-timing guide for those separate questions.

2. Can we describe the operating model and the buyer's role?

State what the business supplies, who pays for it, how the work is delivered, and what the buyer expects to do after closing. Identify the people, premises, systems, permissions, and relationships the model depends on. Mark an unfamiliar or unclear element as a research question rather than assuming it is either safe or unacceptable.

Compare that description with the buyer's capabilities, intended involvement, location, and other requirements. A clearly described business can still fall outside the buyer's current search. A complex business may need additional expertise before a useful screen can be completed.

3. What is the customer concentration, and what does it depend on?

Identify the metric and period: customer share of revenue, receivables, or another defined measure. Check whether apparently separate accounts belong to the same customer group. Keep the calculation separate from the review of contract terms, renewal, payment timing, relationships, and the cost of serving that customer.

A fixed percentage is not a universal purchase rule. The effect of losing a customer depends on the work, margin, costs that would remain, replacement prospects, and cash timing. Record the source and the investigation needed. The largest-customer-loss review provides a more focused downside exercise.

4. What has the seller said about the proposed sale?

Record the seller's stated reason, timing, transaction scope, and proposed continuing role, with the source and date. Ask how the plan relates to the business records and the transition work being discussed. Use discrepancies to formulate questions rather than assuming motives.

Retirement, a new opportunity, or another stated reason does not verify business quality. A difficult operating situation does not by itself establish deception. Keep sensitive personal information out of a general screening worksheet unless it is necessary and appropriately handled. Focus the review on relevant business facts, authorized communication, and the decisions that require support.

5. What funding questions must be resolved before the next step?

Separate the buyer's stated resources, a preliminary financing conversation, an actual financing decision, and the cash required through the transaction and operating transition. Record the source, status, assumptions, and dates. A buyer budget is not lender approval, and a proposed financing structure is not a commitment.

Identify which funding information is required for the next defined decision and who can obtain or interpret it. A gap can mean the screen needs clarification; it does not establish that financing is impossible or that a particular structure will work. Keep the responsible advisers' conclusions distinct from the screening record.

Choose a bounded screening conclusion

Use a conclusion that matches the evidence available. These are record labels for this teaching workflow, not transaction approvals.

  • Outside the current criteria: identify the specific requirement and the information supporting the conflict. Preserve the record if the buyer later revises the search.
  • Clarify before further review: name the material unknown, the next question, and the person responsible for obtaining an answer.
  • Continue a defined diligence step: state the investigation being authorized and its scope. Advancing the screen does not certify records, establish value, approve financing, or authorize signing or closing.

Keep a time limit on initial reading if it helps manage the search, but do not let the clock convert an unknown into a verified fact. Different opportunities require different amounts of work. A screen can finish with an open question instead of a forced yes-or-no buying decision.

Keep the screening record with the source material

Download the blank five-question screening worksheet (CSV). Each question has space for status, evidence reference, entity and period, unresolved issue, next action, reviewer, review date, and the decision being supported. The template includes no prospect or customer information.

Store sensitive transaction material in the buyer's approved systems and refer to it appropriately from the worksheet. Save the criteria version and source dates alongside the record. When a later review changes an assumption, preserve the earlier conclusion and explain the revision.

Use the deal-sourcing guide to retain the introduction and source history. When the screen supports further investigation, hand its material unknowns to the responsible reviewers using the business-purchase diligence guide.

Source and scope

The SBA's business-planning guidance, including its business-purchase section discusses the buyer's resources and capabilities, relevant business information, and a thorough investigation with appropriate professional help. The five questions, decision labels, worksheet, and fictional examples here are original educational tools. They do not represent an SBA assessment, endorsement, valuation, or financing determination.
