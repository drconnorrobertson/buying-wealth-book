Due Diligence Done Right: How to Audit a Business Before You Buy

By Dr. Connor Robertson Published June 24, 2026 Updated November 21, 2026

A business purchase review needs more than a folder of documents. The accounting, legal, commercial and operating reviews need a shared scope and a place to identify dependencies. This guide provides an original agenda for coordinating those workstreams. The word “audit” in the title describes an investigation; this checklist does not constitute a formal financial-statement audit or an assurance opinion.

Define the review with your advisers

The SBA's guidance on buying an existing business recommends an objective investigation and considering help from an attorney and accountant. Its document categories include contracts, leases, financial statements, tax returns and the sale agreement. Use those categories as a starting point for an adviser-defined request list, not as proof that any particular transaction is safe.

Write down what is proposed for purchase, the entities involved, the documents available, the permitted access to people and systems, and the deadlines in the actual transaction documents. Do not assume that a generic 30-day or 60-day timetable applies. Ask the appropriate adviser to identify the scope and limits of their review, including work that has not been commissioned.

Agree the decisions and dependencies

At the start of the review, ask each workstream owner to identify the question they are responsible for answering, the evidence they need, and the other reviews that depend on their findings. Use the actual transaction timetable to arrange checkpoints. A deadline on a project board should identify its source and the person who has confirmed its meaning.

  • Scope: which question is included, who accepted it, and which related questions are outside that assignment?
  • Dependencies: what must arrive or be resolved before the reviewer can answer?
  • Output: what written finding, limitation or open question will the reviewer provide at the checkpoint?

Keep document-level tracking in a supporting register. The separate guide to organizing acquisition document questions by source and review status covers versions, receipt and review status in detail. This agenda uses those records to coordinate the whole review rather than repeating the file-tracking process.

Separate the workstreams

Assign reviewers before assigning conclusions. Financial, legal, operational and commercial questions may overlap, but one person's review does not establish that another person's work is complete. The following are coordination prompts, not a comprehensive transaction checklist:

  • Financial records: ask the accounting reviewer which statements, periods and supporting records are needed and how differences between sources will be investigated.
  • Contracts and authority: ask counsel which executed documents, amendments, approvals and transfer questions require review.
  • Operations: identify the people, systems, suppliers and documented processes involved in delivering the product or service.
  • Customers: ask what supports the revenue and relationship claims, and arrange any contact only within the agreed access process.
  • Transition: record responsibilities, dependencies, outstanding approvals and the evidence needed for each proposed handoff.

Oklahoma State University's acquisition question guide provides broader questions and recommends legal counsel. This article's register and example are original organizational tools, rather than a substitute for that professional review.

Fictional example: a customer agreement is still unresolved

This example describes no actual buyer, seller or client result. A fictional service business supplies a revenue summary indicating that one customer is important to the business. The seller says the relationship will continue, but the workspace contains only an unsigned agreement draft.

  1. Record the claim: “Seller expects the customer relationship to continue.” Do not record it as a confirmed renewal.
  2. Identify the missing evidence: request the executed agreement and relevant amendments through the transaction's agreed process.
  3. Assign the reviews: counsel reviews the contract questions; the accounting reviewer checks the revenue information within the agreed scope. Neither task is complete merely because the draft arrived.
  4. Keep the status open: state what is missing, who owns the request and when the team needs an answer.
  5. Escalate the decision: the authorized buyer and advisers decide how the uncertainty affects the transaction. This worksheet does not prescribe a price reduction, contract term or decision to proceed.

The cross-workstream review makes the dependency visible. It cannot establish whether the customer will remain, whether the agreement transfers, or what protection is appropriate without the relevant evidence and advice.

Review findings before treating the work as complete

At a review meeting, separate resolved questions from accepted uncertainties and unanswered requests. For each item, identify the evidence used, the reviewer, the conclusion and any limitation. A numerical total of completed tasks can conceal one important unresolved dependency.

Ask the team to document which decisions require further information, which items need specialist input, and who has authority to decide. If the timetable changes, update the actual plan with the responsible advisers. A completed checklist does not guarantee the business's future performance or eliminate undiscovered risks.
