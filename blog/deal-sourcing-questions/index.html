Deal Sourcing Questions: Evaluate Leads, Sources, and Handoffs

Ask where an opportunity came from, what supports its fit, and which step the sourcing record actually supports.

Published September 23, 2026Reviewed November 22, 2026By Dr. Connor Robertson

Deal sourcing creates a record of businesses that may fit a buyer's search and the next conversation worth having. A useful sourcing process explains where an opportunity came from, why it fits, which information is supported, and who may take the next step. Keep that record as the opportunity moves from an introduction to a screening review.

Use these questions when comparing your own sourcing channels or discussing a sourcing service. They help define the work and its handoff. They do not establish the value of a business, the accuracy of seller information, financing availability, or the suitability of a transaction.

Questions to ask about each sourcing channel

1. What search criteria does this channel serve?

Write the buyer's intended role, operating region, business model, resources, and material constraints before judging the introductions. Ask the source to explain which facts support the fit. A broad industry label or attractive headline earnings figure leaves much of that question unanswered. Keep the current version of the buyer's acquisition criteria beside the lead record.

2. Where did the opportunity come from?

Record whether the lead came from a public listing, an intermediary, a professional introduction, or contact initiated by the buyer. Preserve a dated reference to the information used. Ask which person supplied it and what relationship that person has to the business. A contact's job title, an email address, or the presence of a listing does not establish all the permissions needed for the next discussion.

3. What is known about the seller's interest and the source's role?

Ask whether a sale is being explored, who can discuss it, and what the intermediary is engaged to do. Record the answer as a statement from its source until the relevant verification is complete. An introduction to someone connected with a company is not confirmation that the owner wants to sell. Clarify representation, confidentiality, and communication arrangements with the responsible people before circulating information.

4. Have we already seen this business?

Compare the business identity and opportunity details with the existing register. The same business may appear in several channels, under an abbreviated name, or with a revised description. Link multiple introductions to one business record while retaining each source and date. Record an identity question as unresolved when the available information is insufficient to match the records confidently.

5. What supports the information in the introduction?

Separate a source's description from a document supplied by the business and from a completed independent review. Note the entity, period, exclusions, and date for a financial summary. A sourcing handoff can identify missing records and assign the next reviewer. It should not relabel an unreviewed summary as verified earnings or a reliable forecast.

6. What is the next permitted action?

Name the person responsible for the next step, the information needed, and the question that step should answer. Possible statuses include clarify business identity, obtain an authorized introduction, compare with current criteria, or prepare a defined screening review. State any unresolved dependency. Keep contact details and confidential records in the buyer's approved systems.

Each record supports a narrow next action. None supplies purchase approval. Distinguishing those stages keeps the sourcing discussion useful without overstating what an introduction proves.

Use one business record with separate source events

Give each identifiable business a stable internal reference. Attach subsequent introductions, updates, and listing changes to it. Retain the original reference and date, so a later reviewer can see what changed and which source supplied the new information.

Download the blank sourcing lead register (CSV). Its twelve fields cover business reference, source channel, dated source reference, stated source relationship, fit facts, unresolved information, duplicate-record reference, communication boundary, engagement reference, next action, action owner, and review date. It contains no prospect details and sends no messages.

Keep descriptions factual and limited to the work. Use access controls appropriate to the information. The public worksheet format does not create permission to collect, circulate, or retain another person's confidential information.

Clarify service scope, incentives, and payment events

When evaluating a sourcing service, ask what the proposed work includes: research, introductions, qualification, scheduling, or another defined deliverable. Ask which activities remain with the buyer and its advisers. Record how the service describes a qualifying introduction and how duplicate or previously known opportunities are handled.

  • Who is engaging the source, and whom does the source represent in the proposed work?
  • What fees, retainers, referral arrangements, or other interests are disclosed?
  • Which event creates a proposed payment obligation, and where is it described in the engagement?
  • What happens if a lead is already known, the scope changes, or the buyer stops the search?
  • What information will be shared, with whom, and under which agreed process?

Resolve these questions through the appropriate engagement and professional review before relying on an assumed arrangement. This guide does not supply a fee benchmark, interpret an agreement, or determine registration or licensing requirements.

Review the channel using defined stages

Choose a consistent period and define the stages being counted. Contacts, source events, identifiable businesses, criteria screens, and diligence reviews describe different work. In a fictional register with ten introductions and three confirmed repeat introductions, there are seven distinct business records. That arithmetic says nothing about whether those businesses fit the search or will complete a sale.

Review the reasons records stopped or advanced: unclear identity, geographic conflict, missing information, unavailable introduction, or a supported next screening step. Keep unresolved records visible rather than recording them as successful diligence. Compare channels using the same stage definitions and review period; avoid drawing broad conclusions from a handful of introductions.

When an opportunity advances, hand over the source history, criteria version, material unknowns, communication arrangements, and next reviewer. Use the business-purchase diligence guide to organize the subsequent investigation.
