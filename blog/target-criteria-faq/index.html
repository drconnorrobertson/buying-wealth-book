Target definition and evidence review

Acquisition Criteria: Set Target Rules and Verify the Evidence

Write down what a target business must fit, which features are preferences, and what still needs proof. A screening decision is permission to investigate further, not approval to buy.

Published September 23, 2026Reviewed November 22, 2026By Dr. Connor Robertson

Acquisition criteria connect a buyer’s intended role, resources, capabilities, and constraints to the businesses they will consider. They help explain why a target belongs in the search. They do not prove that the seller’s financial statements are accurate, a contract can transfer, a manager will stay, or financing will be available. Keep initial fit separate from the investigation required for a purchase decision.

Separate requirements, preferences, and evidence gates

For each criterion, record the reason, how it will be checked, who can interpret an exception, and what would change the conclusion. Avoid a list of attractive adjectives such as “stable,” “scalable,” or “owner-independent” without a testable meaning.

ClassWhat it meansExample questionHow to record uncertainty
RequirementA boundary the buyer has explicitly decided not to cross under the current search.Does the required operating location fit the buyer’s documented region and intended involvement?Record the relevant facts and the buyer’s stated boundary. Do not quietly change it to suit a target.
PreferenceA feature the buyer values but may trade off consciously.Is the preferred mix of services present, and why does it matter to this buyer?Name the tradeoff rather than awarding unsupported points.
Evidence gateA material fact that needs support before a later decision can be made.What supports seller earnings, required consents, management continuity, and the cash needed through transition?Mark the item unresolved until the responsible review is complete. Unknown is not the same as verified.

The classification belongs to the buyer and the transaction team. This guide does not supply universal revenue bands, profit margins, borrowing limits, or industry recommendations. A requirement can be revised deliberately, but retain the old version, the reason for the change, and the person who approved it.

Turn target criteria into an evidence checklist

  • Buyer role and operating capacity: identify the work the buyer expects to perform, the work a manager would own, and what is assumed about the seller’s transition. Separate a proposed staffing plan from a committed one.
  • Location and business model: check where work is actually delivered, which facilities and systems are used, and what the customer receives. A mailing address alone may not describe the operating footprint.
  • Economics and cash timing: keep seller summaries separate from reconciled records. Identify the review needed for earnings, working capital, purchase funds, transaction costs, and the period after closing.
  • Customers and contracts: establish what supports repeat business, what depends on individual relationships, and which contract or consent questions need professional review. Avoid treating a relationship as a guaranteed renewal.
  • People, access, and continuity: record roles, dependencies, expected departures, training, and access approvals. A seller’s assurance that the team will stay is a statement to investigate.
  • Legal and transaction scope: identify the assets or interests proposed for purchase and the advisers responsible for obligations, licenses, leases, and approvals. A screening worksheet does not resolve these matters.

Use the cash-before-first-payroll guide for a cash-timing exercise and the customer-concentration guide for a specific downside review. Keep those analyses alongside, rather than inside, an unexplained combined score.

Common mistakes in acquisition criteria

  • Using a preference as if it were a requirement. This can exclude targets without a stated reason; name the tradeoff instead.
  • Letting a total score hide a failed gate. Favorable location or growth claims do not verify missing earnings support, consents, or funding.
  • Treating seller information as independently checked. Record the source, period, entity, exclusions, and reviewer.
  • Changing the thesis after seeing attractive headline numbers. If a criterion changes, preserve the decision trail and the new assumptions.
  • Confusing budget with financing approval. A buyer’s available resources, a lender’s decision, and the cash required at different dates are separate questions.
  • Failing to define the next decision. “Investigate management continuity” is different from “make an offer,” “sign,” or “close.”

Use a versioned screen and a separate diligence plan

  1. Write the buyer’s intended role, constraints, and current requirements before comparing targets.
  2. For each target, record the source and whether each material item is supported, conflicts with the requirement, or remains unresolved.
  3. Assign an owner and a next action to unresolved items. State the next decision the evidence is meant to support.
  4. Keep valuation, financing, legal, tax, employment, and operating reviews with their responsible advisers. Do not convert a screening result into their approval.
  5. When the facts or buyer’s requirements change, create a new version and explain the effect on previously screened targets.

A useful screen can end with “outside the current search,” “clarify before further work,” or “continue the specified diligence.” Preserve the reasoning even when no deal follows. If the record cannot explain why a target advanced, another person cannot audit the screen.

Frequently asked questions

What should acquisition criteria include?

Start with the buyer’s role, operating capacity, location, business model, resources, and non-negotiable constraints. Then identify which material claims need evidence. The appropriate detail depends on the buyer and opportunity; this guide is a record format, not a universal buying rule.

How narrow should the criteria be?

Narrow enough to explain a decision, with each requirement tied to a real constraint or reason. Review whether a criterion is a preference, a requirement, or a question that needs more information. Record any revision explicitly.

Can a scorecard replace diligence?

No. A scorecard may organize discussion, but its numbers do not establish that records are accurate, terms are enforceable, financing is available, or the operation will perform as expected. Keep the material evidence and unresolved items visible.

What if a target fails one criterion but looks attractive overall?

Identify the failed requirement and who can decide whether to change it. If the current requirement stands, keep the conflict visible rather than compensating for it with unrelated points.

Does passing the screen mean the target is worth buying?

No. It means only that the specific screen supports the next defined step. Price, structure, obligations, funding, and the risks discovered during diligence still require their own conclusions.

Score a practice acquisition target

Take a company that fits your price and geography but depends on one customer for 35% of revenue. Record why the target passes the first two screens. Then model the loss of that customer and decide whether the resulting cash flow supports debt service and a replacement for the owner’s work. Write the answer before the next seller call.
